# The Translator of Silences

Ines had interpreted for presidents and for murderers, and once, memorably, for a president who was also a murderer, and in thirty years she had never been paid for the silences.

The talks were in a hotel in a neutral country, in a room with a long table and two flags and water nobody drank. Ines sat in the glass booth with her headset, turning one side's language into the other's, half a sentence behind, like a shadow that arrived late.

On the fourth day, the old general on the eastern side stopped in the middle of a sentence.

He had been saying something about the border villages. He had said: "The villages along the river, in the years before—" and then nothing. His mouth stayed slightly open. His hand stayed on the table.

In the booth, Ines waited. The western delegation waited, their eyes going to the general and then, uncertainly, toward the booth, as if she had stopped working.

The silence went on for eleven seconds. She counted.

Then the general cleared his throat and said, "The villages are a technical matter. Our experts will submit figures."

Ines translated: "The villages are a technical matter. Our experts will submit figures."

But that evening, in the hotel bar, the western negotiator found her. She was young, sharp, tired, and she asked the question Ines had been waiting thirty years for someone to ask.

"What did he mean? The pause."

Ines turned her glass. "I'm not paid to translate pauses."

"I'm asking anyway."

Ines thought about it. She had heard eleven seconds of silence in many languages. There was the silence of a man looking for a number. The silence of a man deciding to lie. The silence of a man who had heard his own sentence coming and did not want to arrive at the end of it.

"His family is from the river," Ines said. "I'd guess. He started to say 'in the years before the war,' and then he heard himself about to say which years, and whose side of the river, and he couldn't. So he made it a technical matter."

The negotiator was quiet.

"I could be wrong," Ines said.

"Are you usually?"

"About words, almost never. About silences, more often. They have fewer rules."

The next morning, the western side asked for the village figures to be submitted not by experts but by a commission that included residents of the river valley, on both banks. The general looked across the table for a long moment. Then he said: "Yes."

Ines translated: "Yes."

It was the shortest thing she said all week, and the only thing she was sure she had gotten exactly right.
