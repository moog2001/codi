# Lunch Hour on the Moon

My job is to say "copy."

Officially I am Relay Communications Officer, Tranquility Base Annex, but the job is to say "copy." Mission Control in Houston says something, it takes 1.3 seconds to reach me, I say "copy," it takes 1.3 seconds to get back, and Houston knows the Moon heard.

They could automate it. They tried. The automated "copy" tested badly with the public, who apparently found it unsettling to imagine nobody was up here. So there is me, and there is my lunch.

My lunch is the best part of the day. It comes in a pouch labeled CHICKEN TERIYAKI, which it is not, exactly, but it is warm and it has texture, and I eat it at the small window that faces Earth. Earth is blue and enormous. Everyone tells you it's beautiful and they're right, but nobody tells you it doesn't move. It just hangs there over the same rock, the way a ceiling light hangs over a kitchen table.

On Tuesday, Houston said, during my lunch: "Tranquility, be advised, the coffee machine on the third floor is fixed."

This was not for me. Someone had pressed the wrong button. But protocol is protocol.

"Copy," I said, through teriyaki.

Two point six seconds later: "...Tranquility, disregard."

"Copy."

Two point six seconds.

"Sorry about that."

I considered this. There is no protocol for an apology. I could say "copy," but that would mean I had received the apology, which I had, but it felt cold.

"It's fine," I said. "How's the coffee?"

The pause was longer than 2.6 seconds. Somewhere in Houston, a person was deciding whether to answer the Moon about coffee.

"It's okay," they said. "Bit burnt."

"Copy," I said, and I meant something more than copy, and I think they knew it, because the next day, at 12:15 my time, which is lunch, a voice said: "Tranquility, coffee's better today."

"Copy," I said. "Teriyaki's the same."

"Copy," said Houston.

We do this most days now. Nobody has told us to stop. I don't know their name, and they don't know mine; to each other we are Tranquility and Houston, a rock and a city, talking across three hundred and eighty thousand kilometers about whether lunch was any good. It is the most ordinary conversation I have ever had, and I think about it constantly.

When my rotation ends next month, someone else will say "copy." I've written them a note. It says: *At 12:15, ask about the coffee.*
