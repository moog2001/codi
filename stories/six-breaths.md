# Six Breaths

*A short story*

## I.

The wire came up the valley on poles of black pine. Every nine miles it climbed
into a stone tower, and at the top of every tower sat a keeper with one ear
pressed to a brass cup.

Saren kept Tower Eleven. She had kept it for two winters, which made her the
youngest keeper on the eastern Line and, if you asked Old Hadu at Tower Ten, the
most talkative.

The wire could carry a voice nine miles and no farther. Past that, a voice turned
into weather: a hiss, a far-off sea, a sound like sand poured slowly into a bowl.
So the Council had built the towers and put people in them, and the people did
what the wire could not. They listened to what came out of the cup, and they said
it again, fresh, into the horn that faced the next tower.

That was the whole job. Hear it. Hold it. Say it again.

The first rule of the Line was painted above every cup in the same red letters:
NOTHING WRITTEN. The Line carried the Council's words, and words on paper could be
stolen, copied, or left on a table for the wrong person to find. Words in a
keeper's head went where the keeper went, and the keepers went nowhere. They were
chosen for that.

The second rule wasn't painted anywhere, because everyone knew it. Six breaths. A
message came out of the cup, and you had six breaths to put it into the horn. At
seven, the next tower would start asking. At ten, they would ring the trouble
bell, and somebody would climb the mountain with a lamp to see whether you had
fallen asleep or died.

## II.

Hadu had taught her. He had kept Tower Ten for thirty-one years, and he had a way
of saying something once and then looking at you until you understood it.

"Don't carry the sound," he told her in her first week. They sat at his cup
together, sharing the earpiece, their heads nearly touching. She could smell the
smoked tea on his collar. A message came through, so faint it was mostly hiss:

*—ains at the ford—two days—send salt.*

"What did it say?"

"Rains at the ford. Two days. Send salt."

"No. What did it *say?*"

She didn't understand. She said it again, slower.

"You're carrying the sound," Hadu said. "Carry the sound and you carry the hiss
with it. The next keeper gets your hiss and the hiss before yours, and by Tower
Twenty it's a man shouting into a seashell. Listen. What did they mean?"

She thought about it. "The ford is flooding. The caravan will be two days late.
The salt it was bringing won't come, so somebody on the other side should send
some."

"Good. Now say that. In as few words as it fits in."

"Ford flooding. Caravan two days late. Send salt from the west."

He nodded once. "Now it's clean. Now it can go another hundred miles."

That was the craft, and it took her most of the first winter to learn it. You
didn't repeat; you understood, and then you spoke. A keeper who only repeated was
a horn with legs. A keeper who understood could take a message that arrived in
rags and send it on whole. The keepers called it *making it clean*, and the good
ones could do it to anything: half a name, a number torn up by wind, a sentence
that came through as three words and a sigh.

## III.

In autumn the Line carried numbers.

Every granary in the valley sent its count up the wire, and every tower had its
own small piece of arithmetic. Tower Four took out what the river barges had
carried away. Tower Seven added the hill farms. Tower Nine took out the spoiled
sacks, because the store at Nine was damp and always had been. The count came up
the Line like a bead sliding along a string, and each keeper did one thing to it
and passed it on.

*Four hundred and twelve,* the cup would say. Saren would take out the thirty-six
sacks her own valley owed the Council stables, and before her sixth breath she
would say into the horn, *three hundred and seventy-six.* The number would leave
her and go on east, and she would forget it.

That was the part nobody told you about, and the part she liked best. You were
allowed to forget. The number wasn't yours. You held it for six breaths, did your
one thing, and let it go, and your head was empty again by the time the cup
clicked.

She was good at it. She was faster than Hadu, though she never said so. Hadu knew
it and was pleased, and never said so either.

## IV.

Tower Eleven stood where the Line forked. One branch ran on east, toward the
capital. The other climbed north over the shoulder of the mountain to the mining
town at Highfold, and messages from Highfold came down that wire into a second
cup, a smaller one, that hung beside the first. Most days the small cup was
silent. Highfold didn't have much to say to anyone.

In the first week of the hard frost, a rider came up from the capital with an
order from the Council. It wasn't written, of course. He had it by heart, and he
recited it standing in the doorway with snow on his shoulders, the way riders
did, looking at a point just above her head.

"From the Granary Office. This winter the Council will set the valley's share and
Highfold's share together. Tower Eleven will receive the valley's count from the
south and Highfold's count from the north. Tower Eleven will send east the
difference between them, so that the Office can see at once which side has more."

"The difference," Saren said.

"The difference."

"The counts won't come at the same time. The valley's comes in the evening.
Highfold sends whenever it likes."

The rider shrugged. He was a rider, not a keeper. "Then you'll have to keep one
while you wait for the other."

That night, when the Line was quiet, she leaned to the south cup and called Hadu.
Keepers talked on quiet nights. It was allowed, as long as you kept it short and
stopped the moment real traffic came.

"How long can you hold a number?" she asked.

The cup was silent for a while. Then: "As long as nothing else comes."

"And if something else comes?"

"Then you're holding two things." A pause, full of the hiss of nine miles of
frozen wire. "One thing you can hold forever. Two things you can hold for a
while, if you're careful and the second one is small. Three—" He didn't finish.
"There's a reason it's a line, girl, and not a net. They built it the shape of
what we can carry."

## V.

The first count came on a night of wind.

The valley's number arrived from the south just after dark: *nine hundred and
fourteen.* Saren said it to herself once, the way Hadu had taught her to set a
thing down at the front of the mind where it couldn't roll away. Nine hundred and
fourteen. Then she turned to wait for Highfold.

Highfold took its time.

In the meantime the Line kept talking, the way it always did. A message came up
about a lame horse at the Ninth Station. She made it clean and sent it. A wedding
at Low Ashby, the date moved. Clean, sent. Lamp oil wanted at Tower Fourteen. A
fever in the barracks at the river crossing. A man named Tobin Ash who would not
be coming home to his wife, and a sentence about why, which she made as clean and
as short and as kind as she could before she sent it on.

Between each one she went back and touched the number. Nine hundred and fourteen.
Nine fourteen. It was like carrying a full cup of water across a crowded room.
Every message that came through jostled her. Every time she turned to the horn she
felt it slop.

Highfold's count came down the north wire a little before midnight: *six hundred
and forty-eight.*

She reached for the valley's number.

It was there, and it wasn't. Nine hundred and—something. The front of it was
solid; she could feel the nine hundred like a stone in her hand. But the rest had
gone soft, and the new number was standing right on top of it, loud and fresh,
*forty-eight, forty-eight*, and when she tried to hear what had been there before,
she heard forty-eight.

Nine hundred and forty-eight. Was that it? It felt like it. It felt exactly like
remembering.

She was on her fourth breath. Nine forty-eight, take away six forty-eight. Three
hundred. A round number, suspiciously round, and some small part of her flinched
at it. But round numbers happened, and she was on her sixth breath, and the east
tower was waiting. She leaned to the horn and said, *Difference, valley over
Highfold: three hundred.* The number left her, and she forgot it.

## VI.

It was eleven days before anything came back.

The message came up the east wire, from the capital end, which almost never
happened. She didn't recognize the voice. It had been made clean by so many
keepers on the way that it belonged to none of them.

*Granary Office to Tower Eleven. Your difference for the first count does not
agree with the valley's own tally, received by rider. Confirm the figure you were
given from the south.*

She sat with her ear to the cup and her hand over her mouth.

She couldn't confirm it. There was nothing to confirm. The valley's number had
passed through her eleven days ago and gone wherever numbers went when a keeper
let them go, which was nowhere, which was the point. She could tell them what she
had sent. She could not tell them what she had been given. She couldn't even tell
them where she had gone wrong, because there was no place in the world where the
mistake was kept.

That was the worst of it. Not being wrong. Being wrong without a trace.

She called Hadu, though it wasn't a quiet night, and told him everything in fewer
words than it deserved.

"What were you given?" he asked.

"I don't know. Nine hundred and something."

"And you sent three hundred."

"Yes."

"Highfold sent six forty-eight?"

"Yes."

He was quiet for a moment. "Then you heard Highfold's number twice," he said,
"and the valley's only once." He didn't say it unkindly. He sounded tired, the way
he did when a message came through that he had already heard too many versions
of. "That's what it does, girl. The new one sits on the old one. It isn't your
fault. It's what heads are."

"The first wagons went out on my number," she said. She had worked that much out
from the tally the Office quoted back. "Thirty-four sacks to Highfold that should
have gone to the valley. They'll put it right in the spring."

"Thirty-four sacks in spring," Hadu said, "is not the same as thirty-four sacks in
February. Not at Low Ashby."

He meant it gently, as a fact to be carried. She carried it.

## VII.

That night she took the iron nail she used to scrape ice off the horn, and on the
inside of the tower door, low down where the lamp didn't reach, she made marks.

A long stroke for each hundred. A short one for each ten. A dot for each one.

She told herself it wasn't writing. It wasn't letters. It was counting, the way a
herder counts on a notched stick. But she knew what the red words above the cup
meant, and she knew that if she ever had to explain the marks to someone, she
would be explaining writing.

The next count came four nights later. *Eight hundred and sixty-one,* the south
cup said, and she scratched it into the door: eight long strokes, six short, one
dot. Then she put the nail down and turned back to the Line, and the strange
thing, the thing she hadn't expected, was how light her head felt.

She didn't have to carry the cup of water across the room anymore. She had set it
down. When the lame horse came through again (a different horse, or the same one,
lamer), she made the message clean without anything jostling her. When a long,
rambling report came up about a landslip on the river road, she gave it her whole
mind, because her whole mind was free.

Highfold's count came down at dawn: *five hundred and ninety.* She knelt by the
door and looked at the marks, and took five hundreds and nine tens away from
them, and there it was: two hundred and seventy-one. She checked it again. She had
time. She was on her third breath.

*Difference, valley over Highfold: two hundred and seventy-one.*

When the Office asked, a week later, she could tell them exactly what she had
been given.

She noticed one more thing that winter, and it was the thing she thought about
longest afterward. With the marks, she could see herself think. Once, in a hurry,
she started to send *three hundred and eleven* and stopped, because the marks said
something else and the marks were right. In her head, a wrong number had always
felt exactly like a right one. On the door, it didn't. On the door, a wrong number
just looked wrong.

## VIII.

The Line Master came in the spring, the way he did every year, on a grey mule
with a lamp swinging from the saddle. His name was Dornet. He was not cruel and
not kind. He climbed every tower on the Line once a year and looked at
everything, and he found the marks in the time it took him to take off his
gloves.

He crouched in front of the door for a long while. Then he stood up.

"You know why it's forbidden," he said.

"Because anyone who climbs the tower can read it."

"Yes."

"So can I," Saren said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "So can you.
You could read that door now and know exactly what I was given in every count
this winter, and what I sent, and whether I made a mistake. You can't do that
with anything in Hadu's head."

"Hadu hasn't sent a wrong count in thirty-one years."

"Hadu has never been asked to hold two."

Dornet looked at her, and then at the second cup, the small one from Highfold, as
though he were seeing it for the first time.

"Do you know about the western Line?" he asked. "Before your time. Before mine,
nearly. They allowed slates there for a while, for exactly your reason: the
junction towers, the counts, too much to hold." He pulled off his other glove,
finger by finger. "It worked. Then, slowly, it stopped working, and nobody
noticed for a long time. The keepers stopped making things clean. Why would they?
The slate held it. A message came in, they chalked it, they read the chalk into
the horn. If the chalk was wrong, they read it wrong, perfectly, in a good strong
voice. After a few years you could have put a parrot in some of those towers. The
slates said what they said, and nobody in the tower knew what any of it meant."

He let that sit.

"And then someone climbed a tower at night and read a slate about a silver
shipment, and that was the end of the slates."

Saren had thought about the second danger, the thief. She had not thought about
the first, and she turned it over now: a tower full of marks, and nobody in it
who understood them.

"Then make us say it," she said slowly. "Every mark, out loud, into the horn, at
the moment it's made. The next tower hears the number and the mark at the same
time. If what I say and what I scratch ever disagree, they ring the bell. And the
door gets scraped clean at dawn, every dawn, so there's never anything up here
for a thief to read but tonight."

"And the understanding?"

"I still have to make it clean before I mark it. The marks hold the numbers. They
don't do the thinking." She hesitated. "They just let me see it."

Dornet was quiet for a long time. Outside, the mule shifted its feet in the snow.

"I can't change the rule for one tower," he said at last.

"I know."

He looked at the door again. Then he took off his heavy coat, the grey one with
the Council's thread at the collar, and hung it on the nail she had used, so that
it fell across the marks and covered them. He went down the mountain without it.

She didn't see him again until the next spring, when he climbed the tower, took
the coat off the nail, put it on, and said nothing about it at all.

## IX.

On a quiet night after the Line Master had gone, she told Hadu everything: the
marks, the coat, the rule about saying each mark aloud. She expected him to be
angry, or worse, disappointed, and he was neither. He was quiet for so long that
she thought the wire had iced over.

"Thirty-one years," he said finally, "I thought the job was forgetting."

"It is," she said. "Mostly."

"Mostly." He seemed to try the word out. "When I was your age, I'd have scratched
up the whole door."

"What's the most you ever held?" she asked. "For the longest?"

The hiss of the wire, nine miles of it, rising and falling like breath.

"Once," Hadu said. "A long time ago. I held a thing for a whole winter that I was
never asked to pass on."

She waited for him to say what it was. He didn't, and after a while she
understood that he wasn't going to, and that this was the answer. Some things are
held so they can be passed on clean. Some things are held because they are yours.
No door in the world is the right place for those.

"Goodnight, Hadu," she said.

"Goodnight."

Then the cup clicked, and a voice came up out of the hiss, ragged and nine miles
old, and she leaned in to make it clean.
