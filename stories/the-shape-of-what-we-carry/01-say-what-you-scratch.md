# Chapter One: Say What You Scratch

The coat was gone, and the marks were still there.

Every morning that autumn Saren knelt by the tower door with the iron nail and a rag soaked in snowmelt and scraped the night's numbers back into dust. The marks came off white, then grey, then not at all, and the door was only a door again: old pine, black with lamp smoke, a crack along the bottom where the wind came in. Every night, when the counts came, she made the marks again. It had become as much a part of the tower as the cup and the horn and the six breaths, and she no longer flinched when she picked up the nail.

What she did instead was speak.

"Long stroke," she said into the horn, the first time a count came that autumn. Her voice sounded strange to her, too loud in the small round room. "Long stroke. Long stroke. Long stroke. Long stroke. Long stroke. Long stroke. Short. Short. Dot. Dot. Dot. Seven hundred and twenty-three, from the south. Marked."

She had promised Dornet. Not promised, exactly. He had said nothing when he came for his coat in the spring, and he had said nothing as he buttoned it, and he had said nothing as he went down the stairs. At the bottom he had stopped, with the mule's lead in one hand, and called up without turning around: "If you're going to do it, do it out loud."

So she did it out loud.

Nine miles east, Tower Twelve heard every stroke. For the first two months it was old Annick at Twelve, who had kept the tower for twenty-six years and was deaf in her left ear and patient in a way that made Saren feel very young. Annick listened to the strokes the way she listened to everything, without comment, and once, after a long count, said only, *You've a nice clear voice for it,* and went back to her tea.

Then Annick retired to her daughter's farm at Low Ashby, and the new keeper came, and the first thing he ever said to Saren was: "Are you counting to yourself?"

---

It was late in the evening. A count had come up from the south (four hundred and six, which was low; the hill farms had had a bad year) and Saren had marked it and spoken it, and she was waiting for Highfold. The voice from the east cup was young and male and amused.

"I'm marking," she said. "The count. Before I hold it."

"You're marking." A pause. "With what?"

"A nail. On the door."

"That's writing."

"It's counting. Like a herder's stick." She had said this so many times to herself that it came out smooth.

"It's writing," the voice said again, cheerfully, as if they were agreeing. "There are red letters above my cup that say not to do that. Do you not have the red letters at Eleven? Maybe they ran out of paint."

"Who is this?"

"Pell Arran. Tower Twelve. Annick left me a note." He said it with relish, because of course Annick had not left him a note. Annick couldn't write any more than Saren could, or any keeper. "It said, *Saren at Eleven talks to her door. Be kind.*"

"Annick did not say that."

"She said something like it. She said you were clever and you did something odd with the counts and the Line Master knew about it, so I shouldn't make trouble." Another pause. "I'm not making trouble. I'm curious. Why can't you just hold it?"

Saren looked at the small cup, the Highfold cup, which was silent. "Because I have to hold it for a long time. Until Highfold sends theirs. And other things come through in between."

"So? You hold it while the other things come through."

"Try it," she said, more sharply than she meant to.

"All right." He said it immediately, without any sharpness at all, and she understood in that moment that he had been waiting for her to say it. "Give me three."

---

She gave him three.

She would remember that night for a long time, though not, at first, for the right reasons. She would remember it because it was the first time she understood that her limit was not everyone's limit.

She said: "Seven hundred and forty. Two hundred and sixteen. Nine hundred and three." Then, before he could answer, she sent him traffic. Real traffic, from the south cup: a message about a broken axle on the Ford Road, a request from the barracks at the river crossing for winter boots, a long and tedious complaint from the miller at Low Ashby about the price of millstones. She made each one clean and sent it east, and at Twelve she could hear him make them clean and send them on, his voice quick and easy, no hesitation.

Then she said: "The three numbers. Add the first two. Take away the third."

He didn't pause at all. "Fifty-three. Seven forty and two sixteen is nine fifty-six, take away nine oh three, fifty-three." Then, as if it had cost him nothing: "Do you want me to say them backwards?"

She sat back from the cup.

"How?"

"I don't know. I just keep them." He sounded almost apologetic now, which was worse than the cheerfulness. "Three's easy. Four, if they're short. Five once, but I had a headache for a day after, and my mother said my eyes looked strange. My mother had a lot of debts, when I was small, and she couldn't—" He stopped. "Never mind. I just keep them. I put them in different rooms."

"Rooms?"

"In my head. The first one goes in the kitchen. The second one in the loft. The third one sits on the step outside. They don't bump into each other if they're in different rooms." He laughed a little. "It sounds stupid when I say it."

It didn't sound stupid. It sounded like something she had never been able to do, and never would.

"Highfold's coming," she said, because the small cup had clicked. And then, because she couldn't help it: "Listen to this one. Listen to how I do it."

*Three hundred and eighty-eight,* said Highfold, ragged with wind.

"Highfold, three hundred eighty-eight," Saren said into the horn. "From the door: four hundred and six. Long stroke. Long stroke. Long stroke. Long stroke, that's four hundreds. No short strokes. Dot, dot, dot, dot, dot, dot. Take away three eighty-eight. Eighteen. Difference, valley over Highfold: eighteen."

The east cup was quiet for a moment.

"You narrate it," Pell said. "Like a child doing sums at a table."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"So that you can hear it," she said. "So that if I've made a mistake, you'll catch it. You just did the sum in your head while I said it, didn't you?"

A longer pause.

"Yes," he admitted.

"Was I right?"

"You were right."

"Then you've checked me. And if I'd been wrong, you could have rung the bell." She leaned back. "When you do your rooms, who checks you?"

He didn't answer that. After a while he said, "Goodnight, Eleven," and the east cup went to hiss.

---

In the morning, scraping the door, she thought about rooms.

She had tried to put numbers in rooms, after that, the way he described it. She had tried for an hour in the grey light, with the door half-scraped, putting six hundred and twelve in her mother's kitchen in Low Ashby and three hundred and five in the hayloft. But the kitchen was too full. Her mother was in it, and the smell of onions, and the cat, and the number sat down at the table among them and immediately began to blur. And the hayloft was where she and her brother had hidden, once, in a thunderstorm, and when she put a number there it turned into the sound of rain on the roof.

Her rooms were too full of other things. Pell's rooms, she suspected, had been kept empty on purpose.

She finished the door. The last mark came off. She wrung the rag out the window and watched the water fall the long way down to the rocks.

---

The rider came on the first day of the hard frost, as riders always seemed to, and this time it was a different rider: older, a woman, with a scarf over her mouth and a voice like gravel in a bucket. She stood in the doorway and did not look at the door, though it was the morning after a heavy count, and the marks were still there. Saren hadn't scraped them yet. She stood very still and waited for the rider to see, and the rider looked at a point above her head and recited.

"From the Granary Office, by order of Councillor Tavish Orr, newly appointed. The Thornmere branch of the Line is complete to Tower T-Three. From the first of next month, Tower Eleven will receive the count of the Thornmere farms on a third wire, in addition to the valley and Highfold. Tower Eleven will send east the valley's share and Highfold's share and Thornmere's share, each as a part of the whole."

Saren felt the words arrive and sit in her like stones.

"Each as a part of the whole," she repeated.

"The Office wishes to know how much of the total each side holds." The rider recited it the way you'd recite a list of rivers. Then, unexpectedly, she lowered her eyes from the point above Saren's head and looked straight at her, and her voice was her own. "The new Councillor likes fractions, girl. God help us all."

"Who is he?"

"A clerk who became a minister." The rider pulled her scarf back up over her mouth. "He keeps ledgers. You know what ledgers are?"

"I've heard of them."

"Books full of numbers. Thousands and thousands of numbers, all written down. He writes everything. Every sack in the kingdom, every coin, every mule. In the Granary Office they have a room with nothing in it but shelves of his ledgers." She shrugged. "He wants the Line to be as good as his books."

"The Line isn't a book."

"I know that. You know that." The rider looked, for the first time, at the door, at the strokes and dots in the half-light, and her eyes stayed there a moment longer than they needed to. "He doesn't know that yet."

She went down the stairs. At the bottom she called up: "There's also an engineer coming. From Aster. With crates. That's not in the order, I just heard it at the inn. You'll want to sweep."

---

That night, when the Line was quiet, Saren called Hadu.

She called him most nights now, more than she should, and Hadu let her. Their conversations had become a habit, like the scraping of the door: a few minutes after the last count, when the hiss in the cup was low and steady and the whole long string of towers seemed to be breathing out. Sometimes they talked about the counts. More often they talked about nothing: the weather at Ten, the weather at Eleven, a fox Hadu had seen, a song Saren half remembered and Hadu knew all of. Once, he sang it. It was the only time she had heard him sing, and his voice through nine miles of wire was thin and wavering and perfectly in tune, and she had not known what to say afterward and so had said nothing, and that had been right.

Tonight she told him about Thornmere.

"Three," Hadu said, when she had finished.

"Three. Each as a part of the whole."

"What does that mean?"

"I think it means: add all three, and then say how much of the whole each one is. Like—if the valley has two hundred and the total is a thousand, then the valley is a fifth."

"Fifths," said Hadu. He coughed. It was a long cough, and it went on for a while after it should have stopped. "In thirty-one years, I've never had to say a fifth into a horn."

"Are you all right?"

"It's the frost. It gets into the stone." The cough again, briefer. "Can you do it? Three, and the fifths?"

"With the door, yes. It'll be slow. I'll have to mark all three, and add them, and then—" She thought about it. "Dividing on a door is hard. I'll need to think about how."

"And the boy at Twelve?"

"The boy at Twelve could do it in his head," Saren said, "and say it backwards."

Hadu was silent for a moment. "Is that so."

"He puts them in rooms."

"Rooms." She heard the old man shift on his stool. He would be wrapped in his blanket, with his tea gone cold beside him; she knew his tower almost as well as her own, though she had only been inside it twice since her training. "I knew a keeper who did that, once. Not rooms. She put them in colors. Seven was a kind of brown, she said, like wet bark. Four was blue."

"Who?"

"A keeper," said Hadu, in the voice he used when he was about to stop talking about something. "A long time ago. She could hold four, I think. Four, easy. She used to laugh at me, because I could hold only one well."

"What happened to her?"

The hiss of the wire rose and fell.

"Thornmere," Hadu said. "What are their counts like? Big, small?"

She let it go. She had learned, over two years, that there were doors in Hadu you could knock on and doors you couldn't, and that the ones you couldn't were usually the ones he'd stand behind the longest.

"Big," she said. "It's good land, Thornmere. Flat. It'll be the biggest of the three."

"Then it'll be the hardest to hold," Hadu said. "The big ones always are. Be careful with it."

---

The first of the next month came, and the third cup was hung.

It was the smallest of the three, a dented thing of greenish brass that the Thornmere linemen had brought up on a mule along with forty pounds of new wire. They hung it on the west wall, under the window, so that now Saren sat at the center of three cups like a spider at the center of three threads. South for the valley. North for Highfold. West for Thornmere. And east, always, the horn.

The first triple count came on a clear night with stars so sharp they looked cold.

*Six hundred and fifty-one,* said the south cup. Saren marked it and spoke it. Six long strokes, five short, one dot.

*Two hundred and ninety,* said Highfold, an hour later. Two long, nine short. She marked it below the first.

Thornmere came near midnight, and it was a voice she didn't know, a man who spoke slowly and with a Thornmere accent, all the vowels drawn long like taffy. *Nine hundred and fifty-nine.*

She marked it. Nine long strokes, five short, nine dots. Her nail scratched along the pine. The door was crowded now, three rows of marks, and she knelt in front of it with the lamp and spoke as she worked.

"Adding. Six fifty-one and two ninety. Nine hundred forty-one. And nine fifty-nine. One thousand nine hundred. The whole is one thousand nine hundred." She had to make new marks for that, a different kind of stroke for the thousand: a long stroke with a cross through it, which she invented on the spot and announced aloud. "Cross-stroke, for a thousand. One cross-stroke, nine long. One thousand nine hundred."

At Twelve, she knew, Pell was listening. She did not know whether he was adding with her or simply waiting to see her fail.

Then the parts. This was the hard part, the part she'd been thinking about for three weeks while scraping the door each morning. "Valley, six fifty-one of nineteen hundred. That's—" She stopped. She could feel the six breaths running out, though there was no six-breath rule for this, not exactly; the Office had not told her how long a fraction was allowed to take. But the east tower was waiting, and the habit of the rule was in her bones. "That's about a third. A bit more than a third. Highfold, two ninety of nineteen hundred, that's—"

"Seven-and-a-half parts in fifty," said the east cup.

She stopped.

"Fifteen parts in a hundred," said Pell's voice, quick and easy. "Highfold's fifteen in a hundred, near enough. Valley's thirty-four in a hundred. Thornmere's fifty-one. Fifty-one, thirty-four, fifteen. Makes a hundred." A pause. "Sorry. I was doing it while you talked. I couldn't help it."

Saren looked at the door. She looked at her three rows of strokes and dots, her invented cross-stroke, her careful additions. She did the division slowly on the wood, scratching small marks to keep her place, and it took her nearly a minute, and when she was done she had thirty-four, fifteen, and fifty-one.

"You're right," she said.

"I know," Pell said. He didn't sound triumphant. He sounded thoughtful. "But I wasn't sure until I heard you add the whole. I had the three numbers, but I wasn't sure I had the nine fifty-nine right, the Thornmere man talks like he's chewing. When you marked it and said it, I could check mine against yours." He paused. "So I suppose your door checked me, that time."

She sat in the lamplight with the nail in her hand.

"Send it," Pell said. "Your parts. I'll pass them on. They're yours."

She leaned to the horn and said: "Shares, valley thirty-four in a hundred, Highfold fifteen, Thornmere fifty-one. Of a whole of one thousand nine hundred." And the numbers left her, and went east through Pell, and on to Thirteen and Fourteen and all the way to the capital, where, she supposed, a clerk would write them in a ledger in a room full of nothing but shelves.

But the marks were still on the door. That was the thing. The numbers had left her, but the door still had them. She could look at it any time she wanted, all night, and see exactly what had happened and how.

---

She didn't scrape the door that dawn. Not right away.

She sat in front of it with her tea, watching the light come up over the mountain and fall slowly down the pine until it reached the marks. Three rows. A cross-stroke for a thousand. The small scratchings in the corner where she'd done the division, a little tangle of dots like birds' footprints in snow.

It was, she thought, the most beautiful thing she had ever made. It was also, by the red letters above her cup, a crime.

From the east cup, very faint, came a sound she couldn't place at first. She leaned closer.

It was Pell. He wasn't talking to her. He wasn't talking to anyone. He was humming, very low, and it took her a long moment to understand that the humming was numbers. Six fifty-one, two ninety, nine fifty-nine. Over and over, soft as breath. He was still holding them, hours after they'd been sent, the way you'd keep turning a smooth stone over in your pocket.

Or maybe, she thought, he couldn't put them down.

She picked up the rag. She scraped the door, mark by mark, until it was only wood again. And she didn't ask him about the humming, that day or for a long time after, because she had learned from Hadu that there were doors you knock on and doors you don't, and she was beginning to suspect that Pell Arran was a whole house full of the second kind.
