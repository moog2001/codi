# Chapter Two: Rooms

Pell Arran's mother had owed money to eleven people, and she said their names every night before she slept.

He remembered it as a kind of prayer. She would sit on the edge of the bed they shared, in the one room above the tannery in the lower town of Aster, and take her hair down, and as she combed it she would say: "Hollis, the chandler, four and six. Mother Brede, for the rent, nine. Tam at the bakery, one and two. The Widow Fairlie, for the loan in the spring, three, and the interest, which she says is ten in the hundred and I say is eight—"

And so on, all eleven, and the amounts, and what each had been for, and what each person had said and what she had said back. Some nights the list changed. A name came off when she'd paid it. A name went on when she hadn't been able to help it. The numbers moved up and down like the level of the river. And every night she said them, all of them, combing her hair, until he could have said them with her, and then one night, when he was six, he did.

She had stopped combing. She had looked at him for a long time.

"Say it again," she said.

He said it again. All eleven. The amounts. The interest the Widow Fairlie said and the interest his mother said. He even said the parts in between, the *she told me Thursday* and the *he's a decent man, Hollis, he'll wait.*

His mother put down her comb. Then she put her face in her hands and laughed, or cried; at six, he hadn't been able to tell which. When she looked up, her eyes were red and she was smiling.

"Well," she said. "At least there'll be two of us carrying it."

---

His father had been ruined by a piece of paper.

Pell had been too young to remember the paper itself, but he had heard about it so many times that he could see it: a sheet of good cream stock, with a red wax seal and a column of black writing down the center that his father, who had farmed barley on six rented acres outside Aster for fourteen years, could not read.

The man who brought the paper was polite and well-dressed and from the Granary Office, or said he was, and he explained very clearly what it said. It said that the Office would buy his father's barley at a fixed price for five years, a good price, better than the market. All his father had to do was put his mark at the bottom.

His father put his mark at the bottom.

What the paper actually said, as a lawyer explained to Pell's mother two years later in a cold office with a single candle, was that the Office would buy the barley at a fixed price for five years, *or* take the land in lieu if the harvest fell short of a certain weight, a weight that was written in a column his father had never been shown, in numbers he could not have read if he had. The harvest fell short in the second year. It had been a wet summer. Everyone's harvest fell short. But most people had not signed the paper.

His father went to the Office to explain. The man who had brought the paper said he didn't remember saying any of the things his father said he'd said. "What we said," he told Pell's father, "is in the document. That's what documents are for."

His father went home and sat in the barn for a long time. Then he left. Pell never found out where he went. His mother said he'd gone east to find work, and sometimes she said he'd gone west, and once, when she'd had too much of the Widow Fairlie's plum brandy, she said he'd gone to the river. He did not ask again.

So Pell understood writing. He understood it better than anyone he had ever met. Writing was the thing people used when they wanted to be able to say, later, that they had never said what they said. Writing was the thing that let the Granary Office keep a column you had never seen. Writing was a room with a lock, and the people who could write had the key, and the people who couldn't were outside in the rain.

When the Line came recruiting in the lower town, the summer he turned fifteen, he walked into the recruiting hall, and a tired official said, "Can you read?" and Pell said, "No," and the official said, "Good. Can you remember?" And Pell said the official's own question back to him, and then the recruiting notice on the wall (which the official had read aloud to the queue twice), and then the names of everyone in the queue in front of him in order, and what each of them had been wearing.

The official put down his pen.

"Tower Twelve is open in the autumn," he said. "It's a long way."

"Good," said Pell.

---

The rooms had started after his mother died.

She died the winter he was thirteen, of a fever that went through the lower town and took the very old and the very young and, for some reason, her. On the last night she was awake, she said the list to him. Her voice was so small he had to put his ear against her mouth. There were fourteen names by then. The interest had gone up.

When she was gone, he held them alone. Fourteen names and amounts and the things people had said. He didn't know what else to do with them. Nobody came to collect, at first. And at night, alone in the room above the tannery, he would say the list the way she had, combing his hair with his fingers because her comb had been sold, and the names began to blur. Hollis and Tam ran together. The interest the Widow Fairlie said slid into the interest his mother said. He could feel the list going soft in the middle, like an apple.

So he put them in rooms.

He did it without thinking, at first. Hollis the chandler he put in the room downstairs, in the tannery, among the hanging hides, because Hollis smelled of tallow and it seemed to fit. Mother Brede, who had owned the building, he put on the stairs. Tam the baker went in the warm corner by the oven next door. One by one he found each of them a place, a real place he could walk to in his mind, and he put them there with their numbers, and they stayed. They didn't blur, because they weren't in the same place. When he wanted Hollis, he walked downstairs among the hides, and there Hollis was, four and six. When he wanted the Widow Fairlie, he went out into the yard by the well, where she always stood to talk, and there she was, three, with the interest, ten in the hundred, eight in the hundred, both of them, exactly where he had left them.

He paid them all off, eventually. It took him two years of hauling hides for the tanner. When the last one was paid, he went to each room in his mind and took the name out, one by one, and the rooms were empty, and he found that he missed them.

So when the Line gave him numbers to hold, he had somewhere to put them.

---

Tower Twelve had five rooms, if you counted the way Pell counted.

There was the keeping room at the top, round, with the cup and the horn and the window that looked west toward Eleven and east toward Thirteen. There was the sleeping room below it. The store room below that, with the oil and the flour and the firewood. The stair, which was a room if you thought of it properly, a long curling room with forty-one steps. And the yard at the bottom, with the well and the mule shed and the stone bench where Annick had sat on summer evenings for twenty-six years, wearing a dip in the stone the shape of her.

He used them all. The first number of a count went in the keeping room, on the windowsill. The second went down the stairs, on the eleventh step, which creaked. The third went in the store room, on top of the flour barrel. The fourth, if there was a fourth, went in the yard on Annick's bench. He had never needed a fifth since he came to Twelve. He was glad. Five was the sleeping room, and he didn't like putting numbers where he slept.

They cost him. That was the thing no one at the recruiting hall had asked about and he would not have told them. After a long night of holding, his head ached, low behind the eyes, as if he'd been staring at something bright. And sometimes the numbers didn't want to leave. He'd send them east, and they would stay sitting where he'd put them, on the windowsill, on the eleventh step, and he'd hear them humming in the morning as he went down to the well. It took a day, sometimes two, for a room to go quiet again.

He had never told anyone that. He didn't intend to.

---

He went to see her door because he couldn't stop thinking about it.

Keepers got one rest day in ten, when a relief keeper came up from the district post and sat at the cup. Most keepers spent their rest days sleeping, or walking down to the nearest village to drink. Pell spent his first three rest days at Twelve walking. He walked east along the wire to look at Thirteen, a squat ugly tower whose keeper waved at him without coming down. He walked south, into the valley, and sat by a stream. And on his fourth rest day, without quite deciding to, he walked west.

It was nine miles. The wire went on its black pine poles beside the path, humming a little in the wind, and he walked under it the whole way and listened to it, though of course you couldn't hear the messages from outside, only the faint singing of the wire itself. About halfway there was a stone, a rough grey boulder the height of his waist, with a circle cut into the top of it. The halfway stone. He'd heard about it from Annick, who said keepers on adjoining towers sometimes met there, on rest days, to put a face to the voice.

There was someone sitting on it.

She was older than him, but not by much: twenty, maybe twenty-one. Her hair was tied back with a bit of red cord, and she had a heavy grey scarf wrapped around her neck and a basket at her feet, and she was eating an apple and looking at the mountains, and when she heard his boots on the path she turned and looked at him with no surprise at all.

"You're taller than you sound," she said.

He stopped. "You're Eleven."

"Saren. I know. I guessed you'd come this way eventually." She held out the basket. "Apple? They're from Low Ashby. My mother sends them."

He took an apple. He didn't know what else to do. It was very cold and very sweet.

"Hadu said I should meet you," she said. "He said voices lie. Not on purpose. He says you build a whole person around a voice and then you meet them and they've got a different face entirely, and it's better to get it over with." She looked him up and down. "You look like your voice, actually. Mostly."

"What does my voice look like?"

"Like someone who's about to say something clever and is waiting to see if you'll notice."

He laughed before he could stop himself. She smiled, a little, and went back to her apple.

"You want to see the door," she said.

He had not said anything about the door. He had not said anything at all.

"How did you—"

"Because you've asked about it every night for two weeks. Not directly. You ask how the counts went, and then you ask how I did the dividing, and then you ask whether the marks come off easily or whether the wood keeps a shadow of them." She threw the core of her apple into the grass. "They keep a shadow, by the way. Only if you look closely. You can see where the cross-strokes were from the first week."

"I'm not going to write," Pell said.

"I didn't ask you to."

"I just want to understand it. How it works. Why you need it." He was talking faster now, the way he did when something mattered. "You're good. I've heard you make things clean. You're better at it than me—you get the sense out of a message faster, I can hear it, you don't even hesitate. So why can't you hold?"

She was quiet for a while. A hawk went over, very high.

"Because my rooms are full," she said.

He stared at her.

"I tried it," she said. "Your way. After that first night. I tried putting a number in my mother's kitchen, the way you said. But my mother's in the kitchen. And the cat. And the smell of onions. And my brother, who's in the army now, and I don't know where. And the table where my father used to sit, before. Everything I've got is already full of something." She shrugged, looking at the mountains. "I think your rooms are empty. I think you keep them empty. I don't know how you do that."

He thought about the tannery with the hides hanging in the dark. The stairs. The yard where the Widow Fairlie used to stand. The rooms where the names had been, all fourteen of them, before he took them out one by one.

"They weren't always," he said.

She looked at him. She didn't ask.

He found, to his surprise, that he wanted her to. He also found that he was not going to say anything else, and that she knew it, and that she was not going to push. She just handed him another apple. It was the first time in a long while that someone had let him not say something.

"The door's just wood," she said, after a while. "It's not a book. It's not a ledger. The Councillor's got rooms full of ledgers. Did you know that? The rider told me. A whole room in the Granary Office, nothing in it but shelves of his writing."

Pell felt something cold go through him.

"What Councillor?"

"Orr. Tavish Orr. He's the one who wants the fractions." She glanced at him. "Why?"

"No reason," Pell said. But the Granary Office was the Granary Office, and a man who kept a room full of ledgers was a man who kept columns that other people never saw, and Pell knew, with a certainty that came from somewhere much older than the Line, that he did not want that man to have anything to do with him.

"Will you show me?" he said. "The door. Not today. Some day."

"Some day." She stood, and picked up her basket, and put the scarf more firmly around her neck. "Come in the morning, before I scrape it. That's when it's best. You can see the whole night on it."

---

He didn't go that week, or the next.

But he listened. Every count, every night, he sat at Twelve's cup and heard her make the marks aloud. *Long stroke. Long stroke. Short. Dot. Dot.* At first it was irritating, like someone chewing beside you. Then it was useful, a check on his own numbers, the way she'd said. And then, around the third week, it became something else, something he didn't have a word for.

He started to hear her think.

That was the only way he could say it to himself. When Saren marked a number and spoke it, he could hear the number arrive, and then the shape of what she did with it. When she paused, he knew she was looking at the door. When she hurried, he knew she was close to the end of her breaths. When she made a small sound, a little catch, he knew she'd caught a mistake before it left her, and he would wait, and she'd say, "No. Wait. Marking again," and fix it.

He had never heard anyone think before. He had heard people talk. He had heard the results of thinking, the answers, the decisions, the clean messages coming out of the cup. But the thinking itself, the mess of it, the backtracking and checking and catching, had always been inside, in rooms nobody else could see. His own thinking, certainly. His rooms were his. No one had ever walked through them.

He sat at his cup and listened to Saren think, out loud, into nine miles of wire, so that anyone could follow along, and he felt something he couldn't name. It was almost envy, and almost fear.

---

On the last night of the month, a triple count came, and it was big. Thornmere had brought in a late harvest. The valley count came in at eight hundred and eighty-seven, Highfold at four hundred and two, and Thornmere at one thousand three hundred and sixty-six, and in between them, all night, the Line was thick with traffic: a storm warning from the south, a long military message from the river garrison that Pell had to make clean and pass on word by word, a medical request from Highfold for something called *laudanum, as much as can be spared,* which he did not like the sound of.

He held them all. The three counts in three rooms. The traffic passed through him, cleaned and sent. He felt the old ache start behind his eyes around midnight. By two in the morning it was a hard bright line, like a wire pulled tight.

At Eleven, he heard Saren marking. Cross-stroke. Three long. Six short. Six dots. One thousand three hundred and sixty-six. He checked his own number against hers. The same.

She added them. "Two thousand six hundred and fifty-five. The whole."

He had that. He'd added it in his head an hour ago, waiting.

She began the parts. "Valley, eight eighty-seven in two six five five—"

And Pell, in the keeping room at Twelve, reached for the valley's number on the windowsill and found it was not there.

He reached again. He knew he had put it there. He could see the windowsill, the frost on the glass, the place where he'd set it. Eight hundred and—

It was gone. Not blurred, not soft. Gone, as if someone had opened the window and it had blown out into the snow.

He sat very still. The ache behind his eyes was enormous. He could hear Saren's voice going on in the cup, steady, working the fraction on her door. *Thirty-three in a hundred. And Highfold—* He reached for the eleventh step. Four hundred and two. Still there, creaking. He reached for the flour barrel. One thousand three hundred and sixty-six. Still there.

But the windowsill was empty.

He thought, with great clarity: *If I had been the junction tonight, I would have had nothing to send.*

"Shares," Saren said into the horn. "Valley thirty-three in a hundred, Highfold fifteen, Thornmere fifty-two. Of a whole of two thousand six hundred and fifty-five." A pause. "Twelve? Are you there? Pass it on."

He passed it on. He said it exactly as she'd said it; it came through him clean and whole, because she had made it clean, and he didn't have to hold anything at all, only say it again. Then he sat back from the cup and put his hands over his eyes and waited for the ache to go.

It didn't go for three days.

And on the fourth day, which was a rest day, he walked west along the wire before dawn, past the halfway stone, all nine miles, and climbed the stairs of Tower Eleven while the stars were still out, and knocked on the door.

Saren opened it. She was in her blanket, with her hair down, holding a cup of tea. She looked at him and didn't say anything.

"I want to see it," Pell said. "Before you scrape it."

She stood aside.

The door was covered. Three nights of counts, three rows each, the cross-strokes and long strokes and short ones and dots, and in the corners the tangled birds'-foot scratchings where she'd done the division. In the lamplight it looked like a map of somewhere. It looked like a whole country.

He stood in front of it for a long time.

"I lost one," he said finally. He didn't look at her. "Three nights ago. The valley's number. I didn't have it. It was just—gone."

She didn't say anything. She came and stood beside him, and they looked at the door together.

"This one," she said quietly, and touched a row of marks with one finger. "Eight long. Eight short. Seven dots. Eight eighty-seven. That's the one you lost."

He looked at it. There it was. Eight hundred and eighty-seven. Not on a windowsill. Not in a room that might be empty when he reached for it. On a door, in pine, where she could touch it.

"It's still here," Pell said. His voice did something he didn't intend.

"It's still here," Saren agreed.

He stood there until the sun came up and the light came down the door, and then he watched her scrape it, mark by mark, until the country was gone.
