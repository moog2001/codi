# The Last Repeater

The microwave relay on Karsk Ridge was supposed to be switched off in 2009. Somebody forgot to tell the customer.

The customer was a weather station forty kilometers east, a white box on stilts that sent eleven numbers every ten minutes: temperature, pressure, wind speed, wind direction, and seven others that Dmitri had never bothered to learn. The numbers went up to the ridge, through the dish, and down the valley to a server in a building that had since become a furniture outlet. The server was in a back room behind the mattresses. Dmitri had checked.

He was the last lineman on the old network, mostly because he was the only one who hadn't retired, and every spring the company sent him a letter asking whether Karsk could be decommissioned. Every spring he wrote back: *Still in service. One customer.*

Nobody ever asked who the customer was.

The climb took two hours in summer and most of a day in winter. He went up once a month to clean the dish, check the batteries, and kick the generator, which needed kicking. He brought sandwiches and a thermos and sat at the top with his back against the equipment hut, watching the green lights blink. Eleven numbers every ten minutes. Sixty-six numbers an hour. He found it soothing, the way some people find aquariums soothing.

In the third winter after the furniture outlet opened, the generator died for good. Dmitri hauled up a new one on a borrowed snowmobile and spent two days in the hut getting it running, sleeping in a bag beside the batteries. On the second night he couldn't sleep, so he plugged his old laptop into the monitoring port and watched the numbers go by.

Minus twenty-three. Pressure falling. Wind from the northwest at eighteen meters per second.

He sat up.

He opened the logs and scrolled back. The station had been reporting the same falling pressure for nine hours. He called the regional weather office on the hut's emergency line, which still worked, which surprised him, and got a sleepy young man who said their models showed nothing unusual in that area.

"Your models don't have that area," Dmitri said. "That station isn't in your network. Nobody's read it in eleven years."

There was a long pause, the kind young men leave when they're deciding whether you're crazy. Then keys clicking.

"Where are you getting this?"

He explained. The young man said a word that wasn't professional.

The storm came over the ridge at four in the morning. By then the office had warned the two villages in the valley, and the school bus that would have been on the pass road at seven stayed in its garage. It was a bad storm. A roof came off a barn. No one was hurt.

In the spring, the company sent its letter.

*Still in service,* Dmitri wrote. *One customer.* Then he crossed that out and wrote: *Several customers.* Then he crossed that out too and wrote the names of the two villages. After a moment's thought, he added the school bus.
